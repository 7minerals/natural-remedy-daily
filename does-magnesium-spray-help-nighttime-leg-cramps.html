Does Magnesium Spray Help Nighttime Leg Cramps?

Published October 28, 2026 · By Natural Remedy Daily Editorial Team

There is no good evidence that magnesium spray stops nighttime leg cramps. A 2023 evidence review concluded that oral magnesium probably does not help either, and the available evidence does not show topical magnesium meaningfully raising blood magnesium levels. Many people use spray as part of a bedtime routine, but that is personal experience, not proof.

What the evidence says about magnesium for nighttime leg cramps

Most of the research on magnesium and leg cramps looks at oral magnesium, not sprays. A 2023 evidence summary in American Family Physician, written for doctors, asked whether magnesium supplements are an effective treatment for nocturnal leg cramps. Its answer was "probably not." It drew on a 2020 systematic review of 11 randomized trials with 735 people. In people with cramps of no known cause, magnesium did not significantly reduce cramp frequency compared with placebo after four weeks.

For older adults, the Cochrane review of magnesium for skeletal muscle cramps found magnesium is unlikely to provide clinically meaningful cramp prevention. In pregnancy, the picture is less clear. The five pregnancy trials in the review were all at high risk of bias, and their results were mixed, so the evidence there is limited and more research is needed.

Does magnesium spray absorb through the skin?

This is the question that matters for sprays. A 2017 review in Nutrients, "Myth or Reality: Transdermal Magnesium?", called transdermal magnesium scientifically unsupported and could not recommend it. The available evidence does not show topical magnesium meaningfully raising blood magnesium levels. The studies it reviewed were small, which is why the authors called for larger trials rather than saying it has been disproven. Why some people still use magnesium spray at night

Spray is easy to add to a bedtime routine, and rubbing it into the calves is a form of massage. Plenty of people say they feel better after using it. That is personal experience, and it is not evidence that the spray itself does anything for cramps. Customer reviews do not count as evidence either, because cramps often come and go on their own and expectations play a large role in how people feel.

What the NHS says about nighttime leg cramps

The NHS describes leg cramps as common, usually harmless and short-lived, and says most people get them at night or at rest. Possible causes include ageing, muscle strain from exercise, later pregnancy, some medicines such as statins or diuretics, dehydration and liver disease linked to heavy drinking. Some cramps have no known cause. It says stretching or massaging the muscle during a cramp may ease it, and that regular calf stretching may help reduce cramps but may not stop them completely.

When to see a doctor

The NHS advises seeing a GP if cramps are disturbing your sleep, if you also have numbness or swelling in your legs, or if a cramp lasts longer than 10 minutes. Frequent or worsening cramps are worth raising with a healthcare professional, because some medicines and health conditions can contribute.

Who should be careful with magnesium

Magnesium can interact with some prescription medications, and people with kidney conditions need extra caution because the kidneys clear magnesium from the body. Check with a doctor or pharmacist before starting any magnesium product if either applies to you. For the deficiency side of the question, see can leg cramps mean magnesium deficiency?
