Does Honey Help a Cough?

Natural Remedy Daily Editorial · 17 November 2026

Honey probably reduces cough symptoms more than doing nothing, at least in children, but the evidence is low to moderate in certainty. The available research is limited to small trials, and there is no strong evidence either firmly for or against it. This places honey in the 'mixed' tier: potentially useful, but not definitively proven.

What is honey actually doing when someone takes it for a cough?

Honey is a thick, sweet substance produced by bees from plant nectar. Its viscosity means it coats the throat, and it contains compounds with mild antimicrobial properties. The leading explanation for why it might ease a cough is that the coating effect soothes irritated throat tissue and reduces the urge to cough. However, the precise mechanism has not been firmly established by the sources available here, and the research to date has focused on whether honey works, not on exactly how.

What does the research actually show?

The most rigorous assessment available comes from a 2018 Cochrane review by Oduwole and colleagues, which pooled six small randomised trials involving 899 children aged 12 months to 18 years.

The Cochrane review found that honey probably relieves cough more than no treatment at all. That is a cautiously positive finding, but the review is careful about how far it can be taken. The certainty of the evidence was rated as low to moderate, meaning the true effect could differ from what the trials suggest. The review's overall conclusion was that there is no strong evidence for or against using honey for acute cough in children.

NHS 111 Wales lists hot lemon and honey as a self-care option for cough, while noting it has limited evidence behind it. The NHS guidance also notes that a cough typically clears up on its own within three to four weeks.

In plain terms: honey sits in the 'mixed' evidence tier. The signal from trials is modestly encouraging, but the trials themselves are small and the certainty is not high enough to draw firm conclusions.

Does it matter what kind of cough?

The Cochrane review focused specifically on acute cough in children, meaning short-term coughs rather than chronic or condition-related ones. The NHS self-care guidance similarly addresses everyday coughs rather than persistent or medically complex ones. Neither source extends findings to adults, though NHS 111 Wales does not restrict its honey-and-lemon suggestion to children only. No source here addresses whether honey performs differently for coughs caused by different underlying issues, so that question cannot be answered from the available evidence.

Who should be careful?

One caution is consistent across both sources and should be stated plainly.

  • Infants under 12 months must not be given honey. Both the Cochrane review and NHS 111 Wales are explicit on this. Honey can contain spores of a bacterium that causes infant botulism, a serious illness, in babies under one year. This is not a minor precaution.

Beyond that specific warning, neither source lists additional groups who face particular risks from honey itself when used for cough in older children or adults. Anyone with a known allergy to bee products or pollen should approach honey with caution as a general matter, but this is not specifically addressed in the supplied sources.

What does published guidance suggest?

NHS 111 Wales includes hot lemon and honey in its self-care advice for cough, while being transparent that the evidence is limited. Its guidance also notes that most coughs resolve without intervention within three to four weeks, which is relevant context: the natural course of an acute cough means improvement will often occur regardless of what is tried.

Neither source suggests honey should replace any prescribed treatment or that it is appropriate for coughs that are persistent, severe, or associated with other symptoms.

The evidence at a glance

  • Evidence tier: Mixed. Low to moderate certainty across six small trials.
  • Main finding: Honey probably reduces cough more than no treatment in children aged 12 months to 18 years, per the 2018 Cochrane review.
  • NHS position: Listed as a self-care option with limited evidence by NHS 111 Wales.
  • Key safety point: Not suitable for infants under 12 months under any circumstances.
  • Natural course: NHS 111 Wales notes most coughs clear within three to four weeks on their own.

When to see a doctor

NHS 111 Wales provides guidance on when a cough warrants medical attention rather than self-care. While the full detail is in that resource, the general principle reflected in its guidance is that a cough lasting beyond the expected few weeks, or accompanied by other concerning symptoms, should be assessed by a healthcare professional rather than managed at home indefinitely.

Frequently asked questions

Is honey better than cough medicine for children?

The 2018 Cochrane review compared honey to no treatment rather than conducting a thorough head-to-head comparison with over-the-counter cough medicines. The review's finding that honey 'probably' helps does not establish it as superior to other options. That comparison is not firmly answered by the available sources.

How certain is the evidence?

The Cochrane review rated the certainty as low to moderate. This means the finding that honey probably helps is plausible but not firmly established. Small trial sizes limit confidence in the conclusions.

Can adults use honey for a cough?

NHS 111 Wales mentions hot lemon and honey as self-care without restricting it to children. However, the clinical trial evidence reviewed by the Cochrane team involved only children aged 12 months to 18 years, so the research base for adults is not addressed in the available sources.

Why can't babies under 12 months have honey?

Both the Cochrane review and NHS 111 Wales advise against giving honey to infants under one year. Honey can carry bacterial spores that an infant's developing digestive system cannot safely handle, posing a risk of infant botulism. This applies regardless of the reason for giving it.
